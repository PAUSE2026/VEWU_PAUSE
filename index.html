A moment to look back. A moment to choose differently.
What is PAUSE?
PAUSE is a short, self-guided digital reflection experience that invites adults to look back at how they experienced discipline as children, recognize what they may be carrying into their responses today, and consider what they want to do differently.

It creates a gentle entry point into conversations about corporal punishment, positive discipline, and positive parenting, without judgment.

Because sometimes, before we can learn another way, we need a moment to remember the way we were taught. What happened to me? What do I do now? What do I want to change?
Why PAUSE?
Many adults were never taught another way to respond when a child makes a mistake. PAUSE creates a space between what happened to us and what we choose to pass on, helping us reflect on the kind of response, relationship, and memory we want to leave with a child.

The goal is not perfect parenting. It is one different response, one moment at a time.
A complement to the work already happening
PAUSE is created to complement existing parenting, child protection, family development, or community programs. It adds a moment of reflection from the perspective of children, creating a personal starting point for deeper learning and continued change.

Partners can use it before, during, or after existing sessions, or simply share it with families and communities as a resource they can return to. No new program to build. Just a space to pause.
Made to be accessible
PAUSE can be explored in English, Tagalog, Bisaya, Vietnamese, and Khmer, with text-to-voice and video features to make the experience more accessible and engaging.

The website also provides ways to learn more, connect with support, and continue the conversation beyond the toolkit.

Before you begin · The bigger picture
What is corporal punishment?
Corporal punishment means using physical force, even light, to correct a child. Spanking, pinching, and forcing a child to kneel are common examples in our region. Shaming or humiliating a child with words counts too.
How common is it, really This is not just a Philippines, Vietnam, or Cambodia problem. Around the world, most children experience some form of violent discipline at home, and most parents who use it experienced it themselves as children.
National Baseline Study on Violence Against Children, Council for the Welfare of Children & UNICEF, 2016 · Cambodia Demographic and Health Survey, 2021–2022
What the research found Decades of studies link it to more aggression, anxiety, and a weaker bond between parent and child, not better behavior. The one thing it reliably teaches is fear, in that moment.
Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor, meta-analysis of 75 studies, Journal of Family Psychology, 2016
"Effects of Corporal Punishment on Children" · animated research summary citing Alampay, Godwin & Lansford (English) · Watch on YouTube ↗
"Equal Protection for Children" · Global Initiative to End All Corporal Punishment of Children (English) · Watch on YouTube ↗
That is why PAUSE exists. Not to judge you, but to give you what you were never given: another way.
Opening reflection
A moment to reflect Allow 2 to 3 minutes of quiet reflection. You may share if you feel ready, or simply sit with the memory.
Let's bring our minds back to when we were children.
Think back to the home where you grew up. Remember where you used to sit, play, or hide. What did your surroundings look like? What sounds did you often hear, your mother's or father's voice, the television, a radio, footsteps, or noise from outside? Remember the familiar smells of the house too, food cooking, soap, coffee, or the smell after the rain.

Remember the people you were often around. Where they were back then, what they called you, and how it felt to be with them.

Now, from those memories, go back to one specific moment when you were a child and you made a mistake.

Remember where you were, what you did, and who noticed first. Go back to the moment you realized you had done something wrong.

What did you feel first? What happened in your body, did you go cold, did your heart race, did you look down, did you cry?

Remember the person who disciplined you. Listen again, in your memory, to their voice. What words did they say? Was it shouting, strict, quiet, or calm?

And as the child you were then, what did you do after? Did you cry? Hide? Stay silent? Ask for forgiveness? Or promise yourself it would never happen again?

Go back to that moment, and remember what the child you were felt.
Most of us were never taught another way. PAUSE gives you one, not because you are wrong, but because you deserve better tools.
Carry what you just remembered with you as we continue.
Before Step 1 · What is positive discipline?
What is positive discipline?
Positive discipline means guiding a child's behavior by teaching, not hurting. It still says no. It still has rules and real consequences, like a time to calm down, losing a privilege, or fixing what they broke. What changes is how you get there: with calm words and clear reasons, not pain or fear.
Not new. Tested. Programs built on positive discipline have been studied with thousands of parents across Asia, including here in the Philippines, and consistently lower harsh punishment.
Lachman et al., randomized controlled trial of the MaPa parenting program, The Lancet Regional Health – Western Pacific, 2021
"Positive Discipline" · Coram, the UK's oldest children's charity (English) · Watch on YouTube ↗
"The Single Most Important Parenting Strategy" · Dr. Becky Kennedy, TED (English) · Watch on YouTube ↗
Punishment stops a behavior for a moment. Teaching changes it for good.
Before Step 1 · Where PAUSE came from
Where PAUSE came from
PAUSE began with a simple perspective: children deserve to grow up feeling safe, heard, guided, and supported as they learn and make mistakes.

From a child’s point of view, the way adults guide us can become part of how we understand ourselves, how we treat others, and how we learn to handle mistakes. This made us think about something important: what if discipline could help children grow without taking away their sense of safety, dignity, or worth?

PAUSE was created from that question.

It is not a lesson about being a perfect parent, nor is it about telling adults what they should or should not do. It is an invitation to pause, reflect, and discover the small changes that can make guidance more positive.

Because positive parenting is more than avoiding corporal punishment. It is about helping children understand boundaries, learn from mistakes, express their feelings, take responsibility, and grow with the support of the adults around them.

PAUSE is about making that shift together.
From punishment to guidance.
From fear to understanding.
From simply correcting behavior to helping children learn.
From reacting in the moment to choosing a response that helps a child grow.
And sometimes, meaningful change begins with something as simple as pausing.
Step 1 of 5
P
Pause
Stop before you react. Take one breath. Give yourself five seconds before you do or say anything.
The urge to react comes from the body, not the mind. One breath creates enough space to choose what happens next.
In the moment

Your child broke something after you told them not to. You feel the anger rise. Before anything else, breathe.

Step 2 of 5
A
Ask
Ask the child what happened. Let them speak first. Listen without interrupting.
Children often act out for reasons adults don't see. Asking before assuming shows respect, and gets you closer to the truth.
In the moment

"What happened?" Said calmly, not as an accusation. Wait for the answer even if it takes a moment to come.

Step 3 of 5
U
Understand
Consider why they did it. Were they scared? Confused? Bored? Testing limits? Still learning?
Behavior is communication. What looks like defiance is often a need the child doesn't know how to express yet.
In the moment

"What was going on for them?" Not to excuse the behavior, but to understand it before you respond.

Step 4 of 5
S
Support
Respond to the real need behind the behavior, not just the behavior itself. Guide, don't punish.
Children learn better through guidance than fear. A child who feels supported is safe enough to do better next time.
In the moment

Calm redirection. A clear boundary, explained with love. Your presence, not your anger.

Step 5 of 5
E
Encourage
End with something that builds trust, not fear. Let them know the mistake doesn't change how much you love them.
Children who feel loved after a mistake are more likely to be honest, try again, and trust the adults in their lives.
In the moment

"I know you can do better. I'm here." That's enough.

Circle of change
Your story matters.
The next five pages are a quiet space to notice what you learned, what you carry with you, and what you want to pass on differently. You do not have to have everything figured out. Just be honest with yourself.
What is the circle of change? The Circle of Change helps you connect the dots: what happened to you, what you learned from it, how it may show up in your responses today, and what you want to change. It is a chance to understand your patterns without judging yourself, and to choose one small step toward a safer, more respectful relationship with a child.

You are not being asked to judge the person you were, or the adults who raised you. You are being invited to notice what you want to keep, what you want to let go of, and what kind of memory you want a child to carry from you.
